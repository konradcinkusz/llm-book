Everything so far has been an agent. This chapter is about the service around it:
one process holding hundreds of concurrent conversations, streaming tokens to
browsers, surviving client disconnections, respecting a provider's rate limit,
and shutting down without losing work.

It is where Part~I's \code{asyncio} material and Part~III's persistence material
meet, and it is the point at which a demo becomes a system. Two measurements
carry it, both run, both free.

\section{Build the agent once}
\label{sec:lifespan}
\index{FastAPI!lifespan}

The most common defect in agent services, and the easiest to fix:

\begin{python}[caption={\code{lifespan}, not the request handler}, label={lst:lifespan}]
from contextlib import asynccontextmanager


@asynccontextmanager
async def lifespan(app: FastAPI):
    async with AsyncPostgresSaver.from_conn_string(DB_URL) as checkpointer:
        await checkpointer.setup()
        app.state.agent = build_agent(checkpointer)
        yield
    # teardown runs after `yield`: pools closed, work drained


app = FastAPI(lifespan=lifespan)
\end{python}

The connection pool, the HTTP client and the compiled graph are all expensive and
all reusable. Building them per request costs a pool handshake on every call and
defeats connection reuse entirely.

There is a second reason, specific to this book. Chapter~\ref{ch:persistence}
established that rehydration requires graph construction to be a repeatable pure
function. Putting that construction in one \code{build\_agent} called from
\code{lifespan} is what makes it repeatable --- and the same function is what
your tests call.

\begin{warning}
The teardown half is not optional. Code after \code{yield} runs on shutdown, and
it is where in-flight runs get drained and pools get closed.
\S\ref{sec:shutdown} is about what happens if you skip it: Kubernetes sends
SIGTERM, then SIGKILL a grace period later, and anything not checkpointed by then
is gone.
\end{warning}

\section{How far one process goes}
\label{sec:measurement-service}
\index{measurement!service throughput}

Chapter~\ref{ch:asyncio} measured the event loop in-process. This measures a
service.

\textbf{Method.} A FastAPI application under uvicorn, driven over real HTTP.
The provider is mocked at a fixed 100\,ms so that what is measured is the
\emph{service} rather than the model --- a real provider's variance would swamp
the effect entirely. Three scenarios: every await genuine; one request in twenty
calling a blocking library for 50\,ms; and that same offender wrapped in
\code{to\_thread}. Four hundred requests per cell. The script is
\code{code/ch13/bench\_service.py}.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}rlrrrX@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Conc.} & \textbf{Mode} & \textbf{p50} & \textbf{p95} & \textbf{Throughput} \\
\midrule
1 & clean & 103\,ms & 104\,ms & 10/s \\
10 & clean & 102\,ms & 108\,ms & 97/s \\
20 & clean & 103\,ms & 110\,ms & 190/s \\
40 & clean & 103\,ms & 159\,ms & 349/s \\
\midrule
40 & blocking & 164\,ms & 329\,ms & 194/s \\
40 & threaded & 102\,ms & 197\,ms & 332/s \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

Two results, and the first is the one to internalise.

\textbf{Latency is flat while throughput is linear.} From one concurrent request
to forty, p50 did not move: 103\,ms, 102\,ms, 103\,ms, 103\,ms --- against a
mocked provider latency of 100\,ms. Throughput went 10, 97, 190, 349 per second.
One process, one thread, thirty-five times the work at the same per-request
latency. That is the I/O-bound argument from Chapter~\ref{ch:asyncio}, at the
service level, and it is why an agent service scales on concurrency rather than
on cores.

\textbf{One request in twenty destroys nearly half the throughput.} The blocking
row is 5\% of requests doing something wrong. It cost 44\% of throughput
(349~$\rightarrow$~194/s), raised p50 by 60\% and doubled p95. Wrapping that one
call in \code{to\_thread} recovered almost all of it (332/s).

\begin{warning}
Note which requests suffered. The offender was one in twenty, but p50 --- the
\emph{median}, the typical request --- rose from 103 to 164\,ms. The other
nineteen requests in twenty were not doing anything wrong and were degraded
anyway, because the loop they were waiting on was stalled.

That is what makes this defect so hard to find from a dashboard: the symptom
appears everywhere except on the code that causes it.
\end{warning}

\begin{note}
\textbf{A method note, because the first two attempts at this measurement were
wrong and the errors are instructive.}

The first version ran uvicorn and the load driver on \emph{one event loop}. The
driver's own work inflated the server's latency, and the clean baseline degraded
at high concurrency for reasons that had nothing to do with the server. If you
load-test an async service, the load belongs in another process.

The second version put the server in its own process but pushed concurrency to
100, where the \emph{client} became the bottleneck --- producing the tell-tale
absurdity of the blocking scenario showing better throughput than the clean one.
The script now calibrates itself against a no-op endpoint first, and flags any
cell within half the harness's own ceiling. The numbers above are all well under
it.

A load test that does not know its own ceiling is measuring itself.
\end{note}

\section{Streaming to a client}
\label{sec:sse}
\index{streaming!server-sent events}

\begin{python}[caption={The chat endpoint}, label={lst:sse}]
@app.post("/chat")
async def chat(req: ChatRequest):
    config = {"configurable": {"thread_id": req.thread_id}}

    async def events():
        stream = await app.state.agent.astream_events(
            {"messages": [{"role": "user", "content": req.text}]},
            version="v3", config=config,
        )
        async for message in stream.messages:
            async for delta in message.text:
                yield f"data: {json.dumps({'delta': delta})}\n\n"
        yield "data: [DONE]\n\n"

    return StreamingResponse(events(), media_type="text/event-stream")
\end{python}

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}lXX@{}}
\toprule
 & \textbf{Server-sent events} & \textbf{WebSocket} \\
\midrule
Direction & Server to client only & Both \\
Transport & Plain HTTP; proxies and load balancers understand it & An upgrade;
  some infrastructure does not \\
Reconnection & Built into the browser API & Yours to write \\
Use when & The client sends a request and receives a stream & The client sends
  during the stream --- interrupting, correcting \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

SSE is the right default for an agent chat, because the interaction is
request-then-stream. Reach for a WebSocket when the user needs to speak
\emph{during} generation.

\begin{warning}
If streaming works locally and arrives all at once in production, the cause is
almost always a proxy buffering the response rather than your code. Disable
buffering for the streaming route --- \code{X-Accel-Buffering: no} for nginx ---
and check for any middleware that reads the whole body, such as a compression
layer.
\end{warning}

\section{The client goes away}
\label{sec:disconnect}
\index{cancellation!client disconnect}

A user closing a tab mid-response is the normal case, not the exception. The
disconnect surfaces as \code{CancelledError} inside your generator ---
Chapter~\ref{ch:cancellation}'s subject, arriving in production.

\mermaidfig{svc-sse-flow}{%
  A streamed turn, including the disconnect and both policies for it.}{%
  Sequence of a streamed request with a client disconnect.}

There are two defensible policies.

\textbf{Cancel the run.} The user has gone; stop paying for tokens they will
never see. The checkpoint keeps whatever completed, so the conversation is
resumable. This is the right default.

\textbf{Finish in the background.} Let the run complete and checkpoint, so that
when the user reconnects the answer is already there. Right when the work is
expensive and likely to be wanted --- and it needs a task you hold a reference
to, or Chapter~\ref{ch:asyncio}'s garbage-collection trap eats it.

\begin{python}[caption={Cleaning up either way}, label={lst:disconnect}]
async def events():
    try:
        async with aclosing(stream_agent(...)) as stream:
            async for delta in stream:
                yield sse(delta)
    except asyncio.CancelledError:
        await record_partial()        # cleanup
        raise                         # ALWAYS re-raise
    finally:
        await release_thread_lock(req.thread_id)
\end{python}

The \code{re-raise} is Chapter~\ref{ch:cancellation}'s rule and it is
load-bearing here: swallow it and the server will not shut down, because
\S\ref{sec:shutdown}'s drain waits for tasks that never acknowledge cancellation.

\section{One run per thread}
\label{sec:thread-lock}
\index{thread\_id@\texttt{thread\_id}!concurrent runs}

This is the hazard the architecture creates and does not solve, and it is the
thing most implementations miss.

Because state lives in Postgres, any pod can serve any conversation. That is the
property that makes pods stateless. It also means two requests for the same
\code{thread\_id} can run \emph{at the same time}, on the same pod or different
ones. Here is what that does, measured:

\begin{console}
UNLOCKED -- final history on the thread:
    req-2
    A saw 1 messages
    B saw 2 messages

LOCKED -- final history on the thread:
    req-1
    A saw 1 messages
    B saw 2 messages
    req-2
    A saw 4 messages
    B saw 5 messages
\end{console}

\begin{warning}
\textbf{Without the lock, \code{req-1} is gone.} Not interleaved, not corrupted
in some visible way --- absent. Both runs loaded the same checkpoint, both ran,
and the last writer won. One user's message and the entire response to it
vanished, with no error and no log line.

That is the failure to design against. ``Interleaved state'' undersells it.
\end{warning}

\mermaidfig{svc-thread-lock}{%
  The measured outcome, both ways.}{%
  Two concurrent requests on one thread, with and without a lock.}

An \code{asyncio.Lock} is enough within one process and is not enough across
pods. The cross-pod answer is a Postgres advisory lock, which costs nothing and
lives in the database you already have:

\begin{python}[caption={A lock that works across pods}, label={lst:advisory-lock}]
@asynccontextmanager
async def thread_lock(pool, thread_id: str):
    key = hash_to_bigint(thread_id)
    async with pool.connection() as conn:
        got = await conn.execute("SELECT pg_try_advisory_lock(%s)", (key,))
        if not (await got.fetchone())[0]:
            raise HTTPException(409, "A run is already in progress")
        try:
            yield
        finally:
            await conn.execute("SELECT pg_advisory_unlock(%s)", (key,))
\end{python}

\begin{note}
Failing to acquire the lock is where Chapter~\ref{ch:interrupts}'s double-texting
policy is implemented. Returning 409 is \emph{reject}; waiting is
\emph{enqueue}; cancelling the holder before acquiring is \emph{interrupt}.
The lock is the mechanism; the policy is what you do when it is held.
\end{note}

\section{Rate limits and timeouts}
\label{sec:rate-limits}
\index{rate limiting}

\begin{python}[caption={A cap the provider will recognise}, label={lst:rate-limit}]
# Sized so that (limit x replicas) stays under the provider's quota.
PROVIDER_SEMAPHORE = asyncio.Semaphore(settings.model_concurrency)


async def call_model(*args, **kwargs):
    async with PROVIDER_SEMAPHORE:
        return await model.ainvoke(*args, **kwargs)
\end{python}

Chapter~\ref{ch:asyncio} flagged the arithmetic and this is where it comes due: a
semaphore is per-process, so your effective concurrency is the limit multiplied
by the number of replicas. Size it by division, and remember that an autoscaler
changes the multiplier without telling you --- which is a good argument for a
fixed replica count on the pods that talk to a rate-limited provider.

Retry with exponential backoff \emph{and jitter}; without jitter every rejected
client retries in lockstep and reproduces the burst that caused the rejection.
Chapter~\ref{ch:agents}'s model-fallback middleware turns a provider outage into
degraded service rather than an incident.

\subsection{Three levels}
\label{sec:timeout-levels}

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}lXX@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Level} & \textbf{Bounds} & \textbf{Set with} \\
\midrule
Model call & One provider request & The client's own timeout \\[3pt]
Node & One node attempt & \code{timeout=} on the node ---
  \textbf{async nodes only} (Chapter~\ref{ch:persistence}) \\[3pt]
Run & The whole request & \code{asyncio.timeout} around the invocation \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

All three, and each larger than the one below it. A run timeout alone lets a
single wedged provider call consume the whole budget; a model timeout alone lets
an agent loop forever in bounded steps.

\begin{warning}
The node-level timeout only works on async nodes, and says so rather than
silently doing nothing. That is not a limitation to work around --- it is the
reason the ``async all the way down'' rule is a rule. A sync node is a node you
cannot put a deadline on, in a service whose entire job is bounded latency.
\end{warning}

\section{Blocking tools, and the thread-pool budget}
\label{sec:blocking-tools}

\S\ref{sec:measurement-service} priced one blocking call at 44\% of throughput,
and priced the fix at almost nothing. But \code{to\_thread} is a patch with its
own ceiling: the default executor has a bounded worker count, and saturating it
queues calls, which reproduces the original stall one level down and with a less
obvious symptom.

Order of preference: a native async library; then \code{to\_thread} with an
executor you sized deliberately; then, for CPU-bound work, a process
(Chapter~\ref{ch:asyncio}). A tool that cannot be made async and cannot be
threaded belongs behind the queue in \S\ref{sec:long-running}, not in the request
path.

\section{Work that outlives a request}
\label{sec:long-running}

Some agent work takes minutes. It does not belong in a request-response cycle:
browsers time out, load balancers cut idle connections, and a deploy kills
whatever was in flight.

Accept, enqueue, return an identifier. A background worker runs the graph with
the same \code{thread\_id}, checkpointing as it goes, and the client polls or
subscribes for the result. Because the state is in Postgres, the worker does not
need to be the process that accepted the request --- which is
Chapter~\ref{ch:persistence}'s rehydration doing useful work.

\begin{note}
This is also the honest answer to ``can we just raise the timeout''. You can, and
you will spend the next quarter raising it again. The queue is the architecture;
the timeout is the symptom.
\end{note}

\section{Shutdown}
\label{sec:shutdown}
\index{graceful shutdown}

Kubernetes sends SIGTERM, waits for the grace period, then sends SIGKILL. What
happens in between is entirely up to you.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,nosep]
\item Fail the readiness probe, so the load balancer stops sending new work.
\item Stop accepting new runs; keep serving in-flight ones.
\item Wait for in-flight runs to reach a checkpoint boundary.
\item Close pools and exit.
\end{enumerate}

The grace period must exceed your longest expected run, or step 3 is decorative.
If your runs are longer than a sensible grace period, that is
\S\ref{sec:long-running} telling you they belong on a queue.

\begin{warning}
Every part of this depends on cancellation propagating. A single swallowed
\code{CancelledError} anywhere in the request path --- Chapter~\ref{ch:cancellation}
--- turns a graceful drain into a thirty-second wait followed by SIGKILL, and the
in-flight work is lost exactly as if you had written no shutdown handler at all.
\end{warning}

\section{What to take away}
\label{sec:ch13-summary}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item Build the agent, pool and client once in \code{lifespan}. The same
      \code{build\_agent} function is what makes rehydration repeatable.
\item \textbf{Measured: latency flat, throughput linear.} 1 to 40 concurrent
      requests, p50 unchanged at $\approx$103\,ms, throughput 10 to 349/s in one
      process.
\item \textbf{Measured: one request in twenty blocking cost 44\% of
      throughput}, raised median latency 60\%, and degraded the nineteen
      innocent requests. \code{to\_thread} recovered nearly all of it.
\item A load test that does not know its own ceiling is measuring itself. Put
      the server in another process and calibrate against a no-op.
\item SSE by default; WebSocket when the client must speak during generation.
      Buffering in production is a proxy, not your code.
\item On disconnect: cancel by default, finish in the background when the work is
      expensive. Re-raise \code{CancelledError} either way.
\item \textbf{Measured: without a per-thread lock, one of two concurrent requests
      disappears entirely} --- no error, no log line. An \code{asyncio.Lock}
      within a process; a Postgres advisory lock across pods.
\item The lock is where the double-texting policy lives.
\item Semaphores are per-process; divide by replica count. Jitter the backoff.
\item Three timeout levels, each larger than the one below.
\item Work longer than a grace period belongs on a queue, not behind a raised
      timeout.
\end{itemize}

\begin{exercisebox}
\textbf{1.} Run \code{code/ch13/bench\_service.py}. Then change
\code{OFFENDER\_RATE} to 100 --- one request in a hundred --- and predict the
throughput loss before re-running.

\textbf{2.} Deliberately reproduce the flawed method: run the load driver in the
same process as the server and compare. The clean baseline should degrade for no
reason. Keep both outputs.

\textbf{3.} Reproduce the vanishing request. Two concurrent invocations on one
\code{thread\_id}, then inspect the history. Then add a lock. This is the test
that belongs in your CI, not in a notebook.

\textbf{4.} Kill a client mid-stream and confirm your cleanup runs. Then swallow
the \code{CancelledError} and try to shut the server down.

\textbf{5.} Implement all four double-texting policies over one lock. Decide
which you would ship, and write down what it costs the user.
\end{exercisebox}
