\chapter{Asynchronous Python: The Execution Model}
\label{ch:asyncio}

\begin{quote}\itshape
A coroutine is cold. That one sentence accounts for half the bugs.
\end{quote}

\noindent
If you write \code{async}/\code{await} in C\#, you already have the concepts. What
you do not have are Python's defaults, and they differ in ways that turn correct
C\# instincts into wrong Python code. A \code{Task} in .NET is usually already
running; a coroutine in Python has not started and will not start until something
awaits it or wraps it. There is one event loop per thread, no synchronisation
context, and no \code{ConfigureAwait} to reason about --- and one blocking call
anywhere stops the entire process, not one request.

\textbf{Argument.} \code{asyncio} gives interleaving, not parallelism, and the
agent workload is the case where interleaving is exactly what you want. The
chapter earns that claim with a measurement rather than asserting it.

\textbf{Sections.} (3.1) The event loop: single-threaded, cooperative, switching
only at \code{await}. (3.2) The GIL, honestly --- what it does, when it costs you,
and the free-threaded build as a footnote rather than a solution. (3.3)
Coroutines, tasks and futures; why \code{for url in urls: await fetch(url)} is a
sequence. (3.4) \code{gather} vs \code{TaskGroup} vs \code{wait} vs
\code{as\_completed}: four tools with four different failure semantics, in a
table and then in code. (3.5) Structured concurrency as a design philosophy, not
a library feature. (3.6) Synchronisation: \code{Lock}, \code{Semaphore},
\code{Event}, \code{Queue}, and bounded queues as backpressure. (3.7) Async
generators and async context managers; \code{aclosing}. (3.8) Blocking code:
\code{to\_thread}, \code{run\_in\_executor}, \code{ProcessPoolExecutor} --- and
how to decide which.

\textbf{Listings.} The eight core patterns from the source, each runnable; a
sequential-vs-concurrent benchmark with real numbers; a bounded
producer/consumer with graceful shutdown; a \code{Semaphore}-limited fan-out.

\textbf{Diagrams.} \code{async-event-loop} --- a sequence diagram of two
coroutines interleaving at await points. \code{async-cold-coroutine} --- coroutine
vs task lifecycle, side by side. \code{async-gather-vs-taskgroup} --- what
happens to sibling tasks when one raises, under each primitive.

\textbf{Measurement.} Sequential vs \code{gather} vs \code{TaskGroup} over twenty
HTTP calls with a fixed artificial latency: wall-clock median over ten runs.
Cheap, reproducible on any machine, and it makes the I/O-bound argument concrete.

\textbf{Mini-project.} Stage 01: a concurrency playground --- twenty concurrent
requests capped at five, a bounded queue, clean shutdown --- with tests.
