\chapter{Context Engineering}
\label{ch:context}

\begin{quote}\itshape
The context window is a budget you are spending whether or not you are managing
it.
\end{quote}

\noindent
Everything in Parts~II and~III assumes the model can see what it needs to see. In
a real agent that assumption fails quietly: tool outputs accumulate, history
grows, quality degrades before any hard limit is reached, and the bill rises the
whole time.

This chapter makes one claim and then measures it. \textbf{Tool results, not
conversation history, dominate a working agent's context} --- and almost every
piece of advice on this subject is about trimming history.

% ============================================================
\section{What you are actually billed for}
\label{sec:billed-for}
\index{context window}
\index{cost!per conversation}

The first thing to internalise is that a conversation is not incremental. Every
turn re-sends the whole thing.

A ten-turn conversation does not cost ten messages' worth of input tokens. Turn
one sends one message; turn ten sends nineteen. And in an agent each turn
involves at least two model calls --- once to decide, once again with the tool
result --- so the whole accumulated context is billed twice per turn.

That quadratic-ish growth is why an agent that feels fine in testing produces a
surprising invoice in production, and it is why the window is a budget rather
than a limit.

\section{The measurement}
\label{sec:context-measurement}
\index{measurement!context strategies}

\textbf{Method.} Ten turns, one tool call each. The tool returns a realistic
incident-management payload --- about forty fields, of which three answer the
question. Four strategies over the identical conversation, with tokens counted
by the library's own approximate counter. No provider is needed. The script
lives under \code{code/ch12/} and runs in under a second.

Cost is linear in input tokens, so measuring tokens is measuring cost up to a
price multiplier that varies by provider and changes monthly. The script measures
the tokens and leaves the multiplication to you.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}lrrrrX@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Strategy} & \textbf{Billed tokens} & \textbf{vs naive} & \textbf{Final context} & \textbf{Summaries} \\
\midrule
Naive & 43,510 & 1.00$\times$ & 4,350 & 0 \\
Projected & 6,910 & \textbf{0.16}$\times$ & 690 & 0 \\
Summarised & 28,180 & 0.65$\times$ & 1,795 & 1 \\
Both & 6,910 & 0.16$\times$ & 690 & 0 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

Three findings, and the first is the chapter.

\textbf{Projecting tool results cut the bill by 84\%.} Summarising history cut it
by 35\%. The technique everybody writes about is worth less than half of the
technique almost nobody applies.

\textbf{``Both'' is identical to ``projected''.} Not similar --- identical, to
the token. Once tool results are projected, a ten-turn conversation never reaches
the summarisation threshold at all, so the summariser never fires. Trimming the
dominant consumer removed the need for the other technique entirely.

\textbf{The summarised row is flattered.} Summarisation costs a model call, and
the script does not count it. The real gap is wider than 35\% against 84\%.

\mermaidfig{ctx-window-budget}{%
  Where the tokens go, and what each strategy removes.}{%
  Context composition under the naive and projected strategies.}

\begin{warning}
\textbf{The 84\% depends on the tool returning a fat payload.} Mine returns about
forty fields because that is what incident-management and order APIs actually
return; a tool that already returns three fields has nothing to project and the
ratio collapses.

That is not a weakness of the result --- it is the actionable form of it.
\textbf{Go and look at what your tools return.} If the answer is ``whatever the
API gave us'', you have a 5$\times$ cost reduction available and nobody has taken
it.
\end{warning}

\begin{note}
The library agrees with the finding, which is worth noticing. The in-box context
editing middleware's only built-in edit type is called \api{ClearToolUsesEdit},
and what it clears is tool output. When the framework's default remedy targets
tool results rather than history, that is a strong hint about where the tokens
are.
\end{note}

\section{Projecting tool results}
\label{sec:projection}
\index{tools!result projection}

The fix is unglamorous and it is the highest-leverage change in this book:

\begin{python}[caption={Return what the model needs, not what the API sent}, label={lst:projection}]
def project(raw: str) -> str:
    """Return the three fields the model needs to answer the question.

    Everything else is still available -- the model can ask for it by id. What
    it must not do is carry all of it in context for the rest of the run.
    """
    d = json.loads(raw)
    return json.dumps({"id": d["id"], "status": d["status"],
                       "severity": d["severity"]})
\end{python}

The key phrase is \emph{still available}. Projection is not deletion. Keep the
full payload wherever you like --- a store, a cache, a second tool that fetches
detail by id --- so the model can ask for more when it genuinely needs it. What
you are refusing is to carry forty fields through every subsequent turn on the
chance that one of them matters.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}lX@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Technique} & \textbf{Use when} \\
\midrule
Field projection & The response shape is known. Best ratio, no loss of anything
  you chose to keep. \\[3pt]
Truncation with a marker & The response is free text of unknown shape. Say it was
  truncated, or the model treats the fragment as complete. \\[3pt]
Return a handle & The payload is large and rarely needed in full. Return an id
  and a summary; add a tool that fetches by id. \\[3pt]
Summarise the result & Long prose that must be preserved in substance. Costs a
  model call --- price it against just truncating. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\begin{warning}
Truncating silently is worse than truncating. A model handed the first 500
characters of a list has no way to know the list continued, and will answer as
though it saw everything --- confidently. Always leave a marker saying what was
removed and how to get it.
\end{warning}

\section{Compaction}
\label{sec:compaction}
\index{summarisation}

For conversations long enough that even projected history grows, summarisation
replaces old turns with a summary. Chapter~\ref{ch:agents} covered the
middleware's real signature; the design questions are here.

\mermaidfig{ctx-compaction}{%
  Compaction, and the thing it cannot know.}{%
  History before and after summarisation.}

\textbf{The trigger.} Use the fraction form --- a proportion of the model's own
maximum --- rather than an absolute token count, because it survives a model
change. An absolute threshold tuned for a 128k window is wrong the day you move
to a 1M one, and wrong in the expensive direction.

\textbf{What it loses.} Exact detail. If turn three established a constraint
(``never contact this customer by phone'') and the summary does not carry it, the
model will violate it at turn twelve and there is no error --- just a wrong
answer. A summariser cannot know which detail will matter later.

\textbf{The mitigation.} Keep durable constraints out of the conversation
entirely. Facts that must survive belong in the store
(Chapter~\ref{ch:persistence}) and get injected into the prompt, not left in the
history hoping the summariser preserves them.

\begin{note}
Compaction is a second-line technique. \S\ref{sec:context-measurement} measured
it at less than half the value of projection, it costs a model call each time it
fires, and it loses information irreversibly. Project first; summarise only when
projection is not enough.
\end{note}

\section{Prompt caching}
\label{sec:prompt-caching}
\index{prompt caching}

Most providers will cache a prefix of your request and charge substantially less
for the cached part. The saving is large and the mechanism is unforgiving:
\textbf{it is prefix matching}. One changed token near the front invalidates
everything after it.

\mermaidfig{ctx-cache-points}{%
  Why message order determines whether caching works at all.}{%
  Cache-friendly and cache-hostile layouts.}

The rule that follows: \textbf{stable content first, volatile content last}.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}lX@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Order} & \textbf{Content} \\
\midrule
1 & System prompt --- identical on every call \\
2 & Tool definitions --- change only on deploy \\
3 & Retrieved documents, if stable for the conversation \\
4 & Conversation history --- append-only \\
5 & The new user message \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

Three things that quietly destroy the hit rate, all of them things people do for
good reasons:

\textbf{A timestamp in the system prompt.} ``The current time is
2026-08-04T11:02:07Z'' changes every call and invalidates the entire prefix. If
the model needs the time, give it a tool.

\textbf{Per-user personalisation at the front.} Injecting the user's preferences
into the system prompt gives every user a different prefix. Put the stable part
first and the personalised part after it.

\textbf{Editing history.} Anything that rewrites an old message --- including
\api{add\_messages} replacing by id (Chapter~\ref{ch:stategraph}), and including
summarisation itself --- changes the prefix from that point on. Summarisation is
worth its cache invalidation; an idle tidy-up of old messages is not.

\begin{warning}
Caching and compaction pull against each other. Every summarisation rewrites the
prefix and throws away the cache for the whole conversation. That is usually
still the right trade at the point compaction fires --- but it means summarising
early and often is doubly wrong: you pay for the summarisation call and you lose
the cache.
\end{warning}

\section{Degradation before the limit}
\label{sec:degradation}

Context does not fail at the limit. It degrades before it, and the practical rule
is more useful than the literature.

Models attend unevenly across a long context, with material at the beginning and
end weighted more heavily than material in the middle. The size of that effect
varies by model and moves with each release, so treat specific figures with
suspicion --- including any this book might have quoted.

What follows regardless:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,nosep]
\item \textbf{Position matters.} Put the instruction that must be followed near
      the end, not buried in the middle of a long history.
\item \textbf{A bigger window is not a plan.} Filling a 1M window costs 1M
      tokens' worth of money on every turn, and quality does not improve
      monotonically with what you put in it.
\item \textbf{Relevance beats volume.} Ten well-chosen documents outperform a
      hundred retrieved ones, and cost a fifteenth as much.
\end{itemize}

\begin{note}
This is the one section of the chapter with no measurement behind it, and it is
labelled as judgement accordingly. Measuring positional degradation properly
needs a model, a task with a checkable answer, and enough runs to separate the
effect from ordinary variance --- and the result would be specific to the model
version tested. The structural advice above holds regardless of the number.
\end{note}

\section{Tools and memory in the budget}
\label{sec:tools-memory-context}

Two smaller consumers worth naming.

\textbf{Tool definitions are context.} Every tool's name, description and
argument schema is sent on every call. Twenty tools with thorough descriptions
is a meaningful standing cost before the conversation starts --- and
Chapter~\ref{ch:tools} showed selection quality degrades around that count
anyway. The tool selector middleware narrows the set per call; subagent
partitioning (Chapter~\ref{ch:multi-agent}) narrows it structurally.

\textbf{Injected memory is context.} A user profile pulled from the store
(Chapter~\ref{ch:persistence}) and injected on every turn is a fixed per-turn
cost. Inject what changes behaviour, not everything you know. And inject it in a
stable position, or you break the cache --- \S\ref{sec:prompt-caching}.

\section{What to take away}
\label{sec:ch12-summary}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item Every turn re-sends the whole conversation, and an agent turn does it
      twice. Growth is not linear.
\item \textbf{Measured: projecting tool results cut billed tokens 84\%;
      summarising history cut 35\%.} The dominant consumer is the one nobody
      trims.
\item \textbf{Measured: with projection, summarisation never fired at all} over
      ten turns. Fix the big consumer and the second technique becomes
      unnecessary.
\item The ratio depends on your tools returning fat payloads. Go and look at what
      they return.
\item Projection is not deletion --- keep the payload addressable by id.
\item Never truncate silently. A model given a fragment answers as though it were
      complete.
\item Trigger compaction on a fraction of the model's maximum, not an absolute
      count.
\item Caching is prefix matching: stable first, volatile last. A timestamp in the
      system prompt costs you the entire cache.
\item Compaction invalidates the cache, so summarising early and often is doubly
      expensive.
\item Constraints that must survive belong in the store, not in history.
\end{itemize}

\begin{exercisebox}
\textbf{1.} Run \code{code/ch12/context\_cost.py}. Then replace the fat payload
with one of your own tool's real responses and re-run. The ratio is the size of
the prize available in your codebase today.

\textbf{2.} Set \code{SUMMARISE\_AT} low enough that summarisation fires in the
projected run too. Does ``both'' beat ``projected'' then? Decide what that tells
you about when compaction earns its place.

\textbf{3.} Instrument a real agent to print context composition per turn: system
prompt, tool definitions, history, tool results. Most people are surprised, and
the surprise is the point.

\textbf{4.} Put a timestamp in a system prompt and watch the cache hit rate in
your provider's usage reporting. Then move it to a tool.

\textbf{5.} Take a conversation where a constraint was set early, summarise it,
and check whether the constraint survived. Then move the constraint to the store
and repeat.
\end{exercisebox}

\begin{projectbox}
\textbf{Stage 09 --- context engineering.} Tool-result projection across all five
of Ops Copilot's tools, summarisation at a fraction-based threshold, a
cache-friendly message order, and a per-conversation cost report.

The test contract in \code{stages/09-context/README.md} asks for both halves, and
the second is the one that gets skipped: projection must reduce tokens
\emph{and} must not change the answers on the fixture set. A projection that
drops a field the agent needed is a correctness regression wearing a cost
improvement's clothes.

The measurement harness needs no provider, so the cost report belongs in CI ---
where a change that doubles context is visible in a diff rather than in an
invoice six weeks later.
\end{projectbox}
